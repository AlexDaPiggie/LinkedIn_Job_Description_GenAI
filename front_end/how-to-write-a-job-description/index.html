How to Write a Job Description

A good job description does two jobs: it tells the right people "this is for you" and tells everyone else to keep looking. This guide covers what to include, a full example, a checklist and the wording to avoid.

1. Before you write: get the facts

Most weak job descriptions are weak because the facts were never agreed. Spend 15 minutes with the hiring manager and answer these first:

  • Why does this role exist? New team, backfill, growth? What problem should be solved in 6-12 months?
  • What will they actually do each week? 3-6 concrete activities, not a list of traits.
  • What is truly required on day one? If you would interview someone without it, it is a nice-to-have.
  • Team and reporting line. Team size, who they report to, who they work with most.
  • Pay range and benefits. Approved numbers, not "competitive".
  • Location and schedule. Remote, hybrid (how many days), on-site, time zones.

With these answers, the writing is the easy part.

2. The 8 sections to include

Job title

Use the title people search for. "Customer Support Lead" beats "Customer Happiness Hero". Add seniority (Junior, Senior, Lead) and keep internal level codes out.

About the company

Two or three sentences: what you do, for whom, and one specific fact (size, stage, customers). Skip the history.

About the role

Why the role exists and what success looks like. This is the most read paragraph, so be specific: "You will own onboarding for our 200 new B2B customers a month" says more than "drive customer success".

Responsibilities

4-8 bullets. Start each with a verb, describe outcomes or real tasks, and put the most important first.

Requirements

3-6 true must-haves. Name skills and tools, not personality types. If you ask for a degree, add "or equivalent experience" unless it is legally required. Do not ask for 3+ years on an entry-level title.

Nice to have

Keep these separate. Many qualified people skip a post if they miss one listed requirement, so moving extras here widens your pool.

Pay and benefits

A real pay range and the benefits people care about: time off, health cover, remote setup, learning budget, parental leave. Be concrete: "25 days paid leave" beats "generous PTO".

Equal opportunity statement

A short statement that you hire without regard to protected characteristics, and how to ask for accommodations during the process.

3. Full example

Customer Support Lead (Remote, US)

About us

[Company] makes scheduling software for 3,000 dental clinics across the US. We are a team of 60, fully remote.

About the role

Our support team has grown to six agents and needs a lead. You will run the day-to-day queue, coach the team and turn what customers tell us into fixes with product. In your first year, success means a first-response time under 2 hours and a help center customers actually use.

What you will do

  • Run the daily support queue and keep response times on target.
  • Coach agents through weekly 1:1s and ticket reviews.
  • Own the help center: write, update and retire articles based on ticket trends.
  • Report CSAT and ticket trends every week and agree fixes with product.
  • Handle escalations from our largest clinic groups.

What you bring

  • 3+ years in customer support, including 1+ year leading or coaching people.
  • Hands-on experience with Zendesk or a similar help desk.
  • Clear, friendly written English.

Nice to have

  • Basic SQL for pulling your own reports.
  • Experience supporting healthcare or B2B SaaS customers.

Pay and benefits

  • $70,000 - $85,000 per year.
  • Health, dental and vision cover.
  • 4-day weeks in August. $1,000 yearly learning budget.

Equal opportunity

[Company] is an equal opportunity employer. If you need an accommodation during the process, tell us and we will make it work.

About 300 words, every bullet specific, pay stated, and nothing a reader has to guess. Need one for a different role? See the job description templates or generate one from your notes.

4. Wording to avoid

Some words quietly shrink your applicant pool:

  • Hype: rockstar, ninja, guru, self-starter, work hard play hard, fast-paced environment. They say nothing concrete.
  • Strongly masculine-coded words: dominant, aggressive, fearless, competitive. Research by Gaucher, Friesen and Kay (2011) found ads heavy in these words were less appealing to women.
  • Age-coded phrases: digital native, recent graduate, young team, high energy.
  • Exclusionary terms: native English speaker (use "fluent in English"), culture fit, salesman, chairman, guys.

5. Pay transparency

A growing number of places require a pay range in the job ad. As of late 2026 these include California, Colorado, Washington, New York State and New York City, with Virginia from July 2026, British Columbia and Ontario in Canada, and the EU under the Pay Transparency Directive (2023/970), where national rules are still rolling out. Most have employer-size thresholds and exceptions.

Even where it is not required, a real range saves time on both sides and tends to bring more applicants. Ranges that are very wide (like $50,000 - $150,000) look like a dodge, so keep them honest.

This is a summary, not legal advice. Laws change often. Check the rules for where the job is based and where you hire from.

6. Checklist before you post

  1. Title is the term people search for, with seniority.
  2. First paragraph says why the role exists and what success looks like.
  3. 4-8 responsibilities, each starting with a verb.
  4. 3-6 real must-haves. Extras moved to nice-to-have.
  5. Degree asks say "or equivalent experience".
  6. Years asked for match the seniority of the title.
  7. Pay range included and realistic.
  8. Location, remote policy and schedule are clear.
  9. No hype, age-coded or exclusionary wording.
  10. Equal opportunity and accommodation statement at the end.
  11. Under about 700 words and easy to scan on a phone.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a job description be?

For most roles, about 300 to 700 words. Long enough to cover duties, requirements, pay and benefits, short enough to read on a phone. Keep each list to 8 bullets or fewer.

What is the difference between a job description and a job posting?

A job description is the internal record of what a role does and needs. A job posting is the public ad written to attract candidates. In practice, most companies post a job description written in a candidate-friendly way.

Should I include salary in a job description?

Yes, where you can. Posts with pay tend to get more applicants, and a growing list of places require a pay range, including California, Colorado, Washington, New York, and parts of Canada and the EU. Rules have size thresholds and exceptions, so check your location.

How many requirements should a job description have?

Three to six real must-haves, plus a few nice-to-haves in a separate list. Long requirement lists put off qualified people who do not tick every box.

Can AI write a job description?
